**[EN] Intro**

To understand how strange Google's homepage was, remember its rivals. In the late 1990s, search sites were portals: news headlines, weather, stock prices, horoscopes, directories and ad banners, all shouting on one page. Then a page appeared with a colourful logo, one empty box, and two buttons. It looked unfinished. It was, in fact, the most confident product design of its era.

**[EN] 1. One box in an empty room**

The homepage had exactly one job, and it showed you exactly one thing to do. No account to create first, no category to pick, no instructions to read. The emptiness was not a missing feature list. It was a decision: everything that is not searching was sent somewhere else, so the one action a visitor came for never has to compete for attention.

**Principle Card — One Job Per Screen**
Rules applied: Hick's Law · Cognitive Load
People act fastest when there is one obvious action. Every extra element on a screen taxes the same small pool of attention. The behaviour underneath is relief: a page that asks for nothing but the thing you came to do feels easy before you have even touched it.

**[EN] 2. Speed treated as a feature**

Google answered in fractions of a second, and in its early years the results page even printed how fast the answer arrived, like a quiet boast under the results. The point was never the number. When a tool responds before your thought finishes, the tool disappears and only the answer remains. Slow rivals did not feel less accurate. They felt less true, because waiting gives doubt time to grow.

**Principle Card — The Doherty Threshold**
Rules applied: Doherty Threshold · Perceived Performance
Below roughly four hundred milliseconds, people stay in flow and treat the system as instant. The behaviour underneath is trust built by rhythm: ask, answer, ask, answer, with no gap where frustration can start. Speed was not polish on the product. Speed was the product's personality.

**[EN] 3. I'm Feeling Lucky: confidence as a button**

Next to the search button sat a stranger one: I'm Feeling Lucky. It skipped the results page completely and took you straight to the top result. It even skipped the ads Google earned money from. As a feature it was used by few. As a statement it was enormous: we are so sure the first answer is right that we will bet the whole results page on it.

**Principle Card — Confidence Signals**
Rules applied: Confidence · Friction
People borrow confidence from the products they use. A button that offers to skip the list says the list is a formality. The behaviour underneath is trust transfer: if the tool acts certain, users feel certain, and certainty is what brings them back tomorrow instead of trying a rival.

**[EN] 4. The box helps you ask**

An empty box still leaves one hard job: knowing what to type. Google quietly took part of that job away. Spelling slips get corrected with a "Did you mean". Suggestions fall from the box as you type, finishing your sentence with what millions of people asked before you. You are never really searching alone. The crowd's questions draft yours.

**Principle Card — Input Quality**
Rules applied: Input Assistance · Social Proof
People phrase better questions when the interface helps mid-sentence. A suggestion list is guidance disguised as speed: it prevents typos, shortens typing, and steers vague curiosity toward questions the system can actually answer well.

**[EN] 5. The restraint held for decades**

The most remarkable part is what never happened. Google became one of the largest advertising companies in history, and the homepage stayed nearly empty: a doodle, a box, two buttons. The ads live one step later, on the results page. The front door was protected because the ritual mattered more than the inventory: a clean first screen is why the second screen gets visited at all.

**Principle Card — Restraint as Strategy**
Rules applied: Consistency · Restraint
People build rituals around things that stay the same. Keeping the entry screen stable taught billions of people one reflex: open, type, answer. The behaviour underneath is habit protection: every redesign you refuse is a habit you keep.

**[EN] The honest summary**

The empty page deserves its legend, with two footnotes. First, the simplicity is a surface: behind the box sits one of the largest computing systems ever built, and the results page itself grew busy over the years with ads, panels and generated answers. Second, one box for everything quietly trained us to hand every question, including the private and the medical, to a single company. The design lesson survives both footnotes. Win the entry moment with one clear job and real speed. Spend your complexity behind the curtain, where users never have to watch it work.

အလွတ် စာမျက်နှာက သူ့ရဲ့ ဒဏ္ဍာရီနဲ့ ထိုက်တန်တယ်။ အောက်ခြေ မှတ်ချက် နှစ်ခုနဲ့ပေါ့။ ပထမ က ရိုးရှင်းမှုက မျက်နှာပြင် တစ်ခုပဲ။ Box နောက်မှာ ဆောက်ဖူးသမျှ အကြီးဆုံး ကွန်ပျူတာ system တွေထဲက တစ်ခု ထိုင်နေတယ်။ ရလဒ် စာမျက်နှာ ကိုယ်တိုင်လည်း နှစ်တွေနဲ့ချီ ကြော်ငြာတွေ၊ panel တွေနဲ့ ထုတ်လုပ် အဖြေတွေနဲ့ ပို ရှုပ်လာခဲ့တယ်။ ဒုတိယ က အကုန်လုံးအတွက် box တစ်ခုက မေးခွန်းတိုင်းကို၊ ကိုယ်ရေးနဲ့ ဆေးပညာ အပါအဝင်၊ ကုမ္ပဏီ တစ်ခုတည်းဆီ အပ်ဖို့ ငါတို့ကို တိတ်တိတ် သင်ပေးတယ်။ Design သင်ခန်းစာက အောက်ခြေ မှတ်ချက် နှစ်ခုလုံးကို ကျော်ပြီး ကျန်တယ်။ ဝင်ပေါက် အခိုက်အတန့်ကို ရှင်းလင်းတဲ့ အလုပ် တစ်ခုနဲ့ တကယ့် အမြန်နှုန်းနဲ့ နိုင်ပါ။ မင်းရဲ့ ရှုပ်ထွေးမှုကို ကန့်လန့်ကာ နောက်မှာ သုံးပါ။ User တွေက အဲဒါ အလုပ် လုပ်တာကို ဘယ်တော့မှ ကြည့်စရာ မလိုတဲ့ နေရာမှာပေါ့။
